Train Your Brain to do UX design

  • UX
  • Business centric design
  • Jun 20, 2021
  • 5 Min read
Share this article

Most of us who practice in the experience design profession, are products of an education system that was born out of the philosophy of industrialization. This meant that we were taught the same things, the same way and evaluated by a standardized yardstick to determine if we were all of a similar ‘quality’. The impact of this education on the human mind was significant. Children were forced to only strengthen few brain abilities while subduing most of the others (that design professionals needed).

So designers had it rough! They had to fight against an entire system to develop thinking skills that were relevant to design. Most designers organically developed these abilities through exploration and years of practice. Unfortunately, with a lack of an organized system around this, the ability to do design work got attributed to…genetics, creativity & ‘post-it workshops’. Over time the focus on thinking skills slowly diluted and more emphasis came on production skills and software abilities. Remember…

Tools will keep changing and are often different based on the modality you are designing for. Processes are great too but the real meat lies in the ‘thinking skills’ that a UX practitioner needs to learn to employ.

Focussing on building these thinking skills is a must for people venturing out in building a career in experience design. Being able to apply these abilities to solving problems is the real key to experience design and clearly outweighs learning tools and procedures.

Build core design thinking skills

So how does one go about building these skills? Well, let's start with what thinking skills are required. Here is a view of what skills may be required based on where you are with building your project.

Below are quick descriptions of the skills and some exercises you could try to build these.

1. Critical thinking

Ability to engage in reflective and independent thinking. See connections between business problems and the surrounding context and determine what to do.

Ecosystemic landscape exercise: Take one business problem every week. Do not assume the problem at its face value. Ask basic questions to determine what is the real problem. To support your line of questioning, create connections between the various business parameters (business model canvas) and the problem.

2. BIG picture thinking

Ability to look at the entire scope of the task. You may be building an app but …where is the company heading and does making an app really add value? Are there bigger opportunities or problems to solve?

Steer your company exercise: Choose a company struggling in the market. Come up with what you would do as the CEO to steer the company and win against competitors.

3. Analytical thinking

Ability to break down complex problems into single and manageable components.

Break it down exercise: Pick a complex social problem. Try and break it down into smaller parts to understand it better and create multiple & clear subproblem statements.

4. Lateral thinking

The ability for solving problems through an indirect and creative approach. It is not about logic but finding cross-connections.

Exercise: Try these out http://gpuzzles.com/

5. Low functional fixedness (Gestalt psychology)

Ability to go beyond the cognitive bias that limits a person to use an object only in the way it is traditionally used.

Mystery object exercise: Take daily objects and assign an object to each player. Each player has to create a list of uses for their object other than its traditional use. The player with the most novel & unique uses wins!

6. Empathy

Ability to truly emulate and imagine user behavior & personality.

Shadow exercise: Set up a weekly observation day where you shadow people from different professions. Observe keenly on how they speak, think, use tools…with special emphasis on how different they are from you.

7. Idea Inertia

When the brain is not trained to come up with ideas on the go, you may often hit an idea block easily. The ability to get over this inertia is key to drive enough ideas into a project. Time to stretch your brain like taffy!

80 ideas exercise: On Day-1take a problem and come up with 10 unique ideas. On Day-2 come up with 20 new unique ideas for the same problem. On Day-3 come up with 40. One Day-4 come up with 80.

8. Future Thinking

Ability to create a well-informed view of future lifestyles that can define opportunities for products or services.

Junk Imagination Exercise: Collect a pile of small home objects/toys etc. Pick the future context for an industry and time frame. Depict the future with any of the collected objects. The objects chosen will help you rationalize your decisions on how you see the future. You could write a blog to accompany your junk objects future model.

9. Simplicity Distillation

Ability to clearly focus on what is truly delivering value in the context of technology and behavior.

Distillation exercise: Take an existing process (maybe a service). Remodel the process to be aligned to user expectations and how they would like to move across touchpoints. Cut fat, remove excess and create the cleanest and simplest model of the process.

10. Zoom in zoom out

Ability to quickly shift between strategic and detailed perspectives.

Zoom in zoom out exercise: Take a screen flow from an app. At each screen stop and zoom out on(think about) how that screen supports the strategic objectives of the company and then zoom into the details of the controls and how they support the interactions required in the particular moment of usage. Evaluate a few screens in this way.

11. Narrative Flow

Ability to perceive and imagine experiences as a continuous flow of sensations and cognitive processing.

Experience narrative exercise: Imagine creating the experience of using an app like you were making a movie. Craft and design each frame of the experience and not just screens pasted together. Play your movie through a storyboard that depicts how your experience design succeeds.

These are some thinking skills I see come to play on a regular basis. Here is a summary of why you need to work hard on them…

  • Tools & procedures don't make you a design expert, being highly trained on thinking abilities do.

  • You have a legacy of a bad education system to get over.

  • The industry needs to value/respect designers for their powerful thinking abilities.

  • These powerful abilities can be applied to all types of business problems to create great value.

  • Design work needs to be increasingly less associated with transient tools and procedures.

Have fun trying these! Do comment on this post on how you go about powering your experience design work and what thinking abilities do you employ.
