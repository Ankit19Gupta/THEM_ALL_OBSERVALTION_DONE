Stop Building Catalogs. Start Building Experiences. Engineering, Business, and UX Must Work Together.

The Car Dashboard Analogy

Early cars had a dashboard where everything was shoved right in front of the driver — the gear shift, speedometer, controls — because it was the technically easiest way to build. The engine was in front, so the gear lever stayed close. Wires were short, controls were accessible, and it all worked… sort of.

But it didn’t feel right.

As car design evolved, components moved — gear shifters went to the center, infotainment systems became smarter, controls more intuitive. Not because engineers couldn’t make it the old way, but because drivers expected more.

Digital Products Are Facing the Same Evolution

Today, many digital products are still being built like those early dashboards. Built with engineering-first thinking, or worse — built as a feature catalog:

  • “We have 20 services. Let’s wrap them in a UI and ship it.”

  • “Our product offers A, B, C, D… let’s showcase all of them.”

This is where the product becomes technically complete, but experientially fractured.

It’s functional — but not fluid.

It’s available — but not adoptable.

And that’s a problem.

UX Enters: Designing for Human Behaviour

UX is not just about aesthetics. It’s about orchestrating behaviour, removing friction, and amplifying user intent.

UX asks:

  • What does the user need right now?

  • How do they think about this task?

  • What emotional or cognitive state are they in?

Just like modern car dashboards now anticipate driver needs, good UX anticipates user needs, and guides the journey accordingly.

But Wait — What About the Business?

This is not about putting the user above all else blindly.

Real UX design marries user psyche with business goals.

It aligns user needs with the business intent — be it revenue, retention, or reach. UX isn’t just a user advocate — it’s also a business strategist.

  • If engineering asks how can we build it,

  • UX asks how will users behave with it,

  • And business asks how will this grow us?

The sweet spot?

Products where all three — tech, behaviour, and business — come together.

Real-Life Examples

Fintech App

Engineering says: "Let’s show all services: loans, insurance, credit cards."

Business says: “Let’s push high-margin products.”

UX says: “Let’s design flows based on financial moments: payday, urgent expense, savings goal — and map business nudges accordingly.”

A B2B SaaS Tool

Engineering builds modular access to reports, dashboards, and controls.

Business wants to upsell enterprise tiers.

UX builds contextual value prompts at key moments in usage — right when the user is hitting limits or seeing wins.

Healthcare Platform

Engineering stitches together prescriptions, diagnostics, and doctor bookings.

Business wants more repeat visits.

UX designs for continuity of care — guiding users across the full journey, making repeat interactions frictionless.

From Catalogs to Ecosystems

What you’re really designing is not a bundle of services.

It’s an ecosystem of intent — where users do what they came to do, and the business grows as a result.

UX is the glue that connects the two.

The Final Word

Most products today are either:

  • Engineering-led (functional but fragmented)

  • Business-led (pushing goals but ignoring users)

  • UX-led (pretty but sometimes hard to scale)

Great products are where all three sit at the table — from day one.

So the next time you’re launching something new, ask yourself:

  • Are we building what’s easiest to code or what’s most natural to use?

  • Are we just listing features or guiding behaviour?

  • Are our business goals visible inside the user experience — or outside of it?

Because in a world of infinite options, people don’t just choose based on features.They choose based on how it feels to win with your product.

And that’s not engineering.

That’s Experience by Design.
